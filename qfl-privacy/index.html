    Back to the questions

    The toy is a teaching instrument; it runs the same circuit family at one qubit, and it says so on screen.

    With as many effective parameters as inputs, other inputs often fit exactly

    With one repetition and one trainable block, the best restart matched the gradient exactly on every seed, but on a different input for of seeds at 4 qubits and of at 8 qubits. Another restart sometimes found the true input, but both fit exactly, so the attacker could not tell them apart.

    Back to the questions

    Equal counts of equations and unknowns already allow a second exact solution.

    An effective parameter is one whose gradient can be nonzero

    The circuit has P = 2rℓN angles. The last block's Z rotations are followed only by CNOTs and a Z measurement, so they never change the output; the other P − N angles are the effective parameters. With one repetition and one trainable block (r = ℓ = 1) that leaves N equations for N inputs.

    Back to the questions

    A test in the repository checks, on several shapes, that exactly these angles have nonzero gradient.

    Without the label, the attacker fits each input's weight in closed form

    With a squared loss, input i adds ci ∇θ f(xi) to the gradient, with an unknown weight ci. For each guess, the attacker solves for the best weights (a small least-squares problem) and matches what is left. Hiding the label left seeds stuck on the 8-qubit 1 × 2 circuit at batch size two, against with it: a harder search, not a hidden input. Trying all 2B labelings includes the known-label attack.

    Back to the questions

    At batch size two, four labelings: at most four times the restarts.

    Training: no net change, and one lead on low-frequency curves

    Pooled over the tasks, seeds were recovered only before training and only after (p = ). On low-frequency curves the counts were and , but that is also the task every circuit fit best (test accuracy or more), so a better fit may explain it. One dataset, one example per seed: a lead to test.

    Back to the questions

    Kumar et al. saw a low-frequency target make privacy worse; this lead points the other way, with a different setup.

    What these results do not show

    • Scale. Simulated circuits of 4 and 8 qubits: where each claim holds at this size, not how it grows.
    • Device noise. Shot noise only; gate and readout noise are future work.
    • The input range. The attacker starts inside [0, π) but does not restrict its search to it, so a domain-aware attacker could reject some ambiguous answers.
    • Partial leaks. An ambiguous batch is scored by its worst-matched input; how many of its inputs were still recovered was not measured.
    • Training. One optimizer, small tasks, one training example's gradient per seed.
