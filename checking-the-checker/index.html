Checking the Checker

Six silent failures in an automated citation verifier

November 2026

Hi, I'm AJ, a first-year PhD student here. This talk is about a tool I wrote to check my papers' references, and six times it failed without telling me. If a term isn't clear, stop me. Let me start with what a different tool printed.
1 · The point

A clean report counts only if the checker can fail and says what it skipped

What I did: traced six silent failures in my own citation checker, sorted them into three groups with a guard for each, and tested two guards on five public checkers.

Why you should care: your tests, CI and linters say "all clear" too. If a tool can't fail, "all clear" tells you nothing.

Real output, excerpt, command shortened: HalRef v0.1.0, given my paper's PDF cut to its first 2,000 bytes, October 2026.

This is what a public citation checker printed for a PDF cut to its first 2,000 bytes. One line says it found nothing, and a later line says everything passed anyway. That's my one point today. A clean report counts only if the checker can fail, and tells you what it skipped. Here's what I did. In four weeks of use, I found six times my own checker had failed silently. I sorted those failures into three groups, found a guard for each, and tested two of the guards on five public checkers. Here's why you should care. Think of the last green check you saw in your CI. How do you know it ran? If a tool can't fail, all clear tells you nothing. So why citation checkers?
2 · Why it matters

AI models still invent references, and a top conference now checks them with software

So the trust moves to the checker.

Sources: Xu et al., GhostCite, 2026 (first two panels); ACL 2026 program chairs' statement.

Language models still invent references. Across thirteen recent models, between 14 and 95 percent of the citations they were asked for were invalid. Some reach print: at eight AI and security venues, the share of accepted papers with an invalid citation went from under one percent to 1.61 percent in 2025. One top conference turned to software: ACL 2026 ran an automated checker, and it flagged over a hundred accepted papers, confirmed by hand, that cited work that doesn't exist. So the trust moves to the checker, and I looked hard at my own.
3 · My checker

Six silent failures in my own checks; one let a broken paper pass for days

My checker, dokimasia, checks one bibliography of entries that of my papers share.

Bar: days from the commit that let a run go wrong to its fix (case 3: how long the flawed score existed), from the repository history.

My checker is called dokimasia. It checks one bibliography of 103 entries that seven of my papers share. Each bar is one silent failure, and its length is how long it lasted. The worst was the first. One paper looked for its bibliography in the wrong folder. All of its citations came out as question marks, and its reference list was empty. But the build said success, because the build script ignored the error. That lasted 71 days, and my checks only existed for the last 25 of them. Every time, the output looked valid. This is one tool, studied by the person who built it, so it shows these failures happen, not how often; later I check five other tools. Lined up, my six fell into three groups.

A test of a check runs the check, so it can never see a check that didn't run.A test can catch wrong inputs and misreported results.

A case sits at the first step whose output was wrong.

Every check does three things: it chooses what to check, it reads the inputs, and it reports a result. Each group is a failure at one of those steps. First, did not run. In cases 1 and 2, the check never looked at the broken paper, so it had nothing to complain about. Second, wrong inputs. In cases 3, 4 and 5, the check ran but read the wrong thing, like reading only one of the two dates a publisher database keeps for the same article. Third, misreported. In case 6, arXiv, the big preprint server, refused requests sent too fast, and my code reported each refusal as a missing entry. Each group gets its own guard. For did not run, the checker works out what to check by itself, and names what it skipped. For wrong inputs, break the check on purpose and make sure it fails. For misreported, report no answer apart from not found. A test can only reach the last two: a test of a check runs the check, so it never sees a check that didn't run.
3 · My checker

Today's tests caught of small changes that break my six fixes again

One change at a time, at techne commit dae4ca4. Paper term: fault injection.

To test the guards today, I wrote eleven small changes that each break one of my six fixes again, and ran the tests after each. For cases 1 and 2, a change breaks the guard that replaced the old defect, like the build gate or the code that works out what to check; the old defects were outside the tool, so a test of the tool can't reach them. Each column is one change, and its height is how many tests failed. Nine of the eleven were caught. Two got through. One let the check pass a paper whose compiled bibliography was empty. The other removed the line that says a server was refusing requests. Both have tests now. So I asked whether other checkers have the same gaps.

A checker's all-clear counts only if it can fail and names what it skipped. My own checker failed silently six times, in three groups, each with its own guard: work out what to check, break each check on purpose, and keep no answer apart from not found. When I broke my fixes again, today's tests caught nine of eleven. That shows the tests hold, not that the guards find new bugs. And three of five public checkers printed a clean result after checking nothing. Two problems stay open. These cases show the failures happen, not how often. Isn't this just ordinary bugs? What is new

The defects are familiar

An ignored error code, a list kept by hand, a catch-all exception handler, a cache with no format version. Yuan et al.: 92% of catastrophic failures in five distributed systems came from mishandled non-fatal errors.

The cost is not

Each one makes a false claim about someone else's document: "unresolved" tells a program chair an author may have cited something that does not exist. Registries keep two dates for one article, and a refusal looks like a missing entry.

Closest work

Hill (2026) mutation-tests a verifier that passed a bundle without examining anything, a "vacuous pass". This paper adds the two groups that are not vacuous passes, and tests two guards on five other checkers.

The defects are familiar to any software engineer; what's specific is that each one makes a false claim about someone else's document. Closest work is Hill 2026, on vacuous passes; I add the other two groups and test guards on five other tools.

One tool, studied by its author: what that can and cannot show

It shows the groups occur

A missing guard needs one counterexample: a tool that prints "All Clear!" for an unreadable PDF does so whatever the paper. In their public code, issues or commits, each group appears in at least two of the five other tools.

It cannot show how often

Six is how many were noticed, and the three groups are a reading of them, not a tested taxonomy. The other tools ran once per input, and the network cut was total, so one server refusing requests was not tested on them.

Agreed, one system can't show how often. It can show the groups occur, and a missing guard needs only one counterexample.

Why a test cannot catch a check that never ran

The test runs the check

So it always sees the check run. Cases 1 and 2 left out a file or a check; no mutation exposes them, because there is no refusal to delete.

The guard: work it out, name the gaps

The checker now finds every file with a \documentclass instead of reading a hand-kept list, and names each document it could not check. That code can be broken and tested, and the re-break caught all three changes to it.

A seventh failure, found after the study, is still open: no workflow ran the rendered check at all, so a stale local build missed of citations. Its guard belongs in the workflow, where no test of the tool reaches.

Case 7 is still open: 55 days on October 19th.

At a 2% fabrication rate, most flags are false even for a precise verifier

Most flags are already false

Fabricated entries are rare: if 2% were fabricated, the most precise independent verifier Kim et al. tested would be right on about one flag in six.

A checker's own failures add more

Badalova and Mayr advise checking by hand every reference marked "not found". Case 6 shows that label is not always a finding: the server refused, and the checker called it missing.

At a 2 percent base rate, about five in six flags are false even for the most precise verifier; a checker's own failures add to them.

Case 6: the count moved with the run, not with the bibliography

arXiv was refusing requests sent close together (HTTP 406), and the fetch caught every exception and returned nothing, so a refusal, a timeout and a missing identifier looked the same. The last run, on the fixed code, waited out each refusal, resolved more and gave a reason for each of the left: all refused. Two weeks later, with no identifier changed, a scheduled run left none unresolved.

Each failure now carries its reason, the summary says when a host was throttling, and failed lookups are never cached.

Cut for time: cases 3 to 5 up close

3 · A score that rewarded citing everything

Coverage counted all entries in the shared file. A skeleton citing everything scored 100%; a real paper citing 20 scored % and failed. Now it compares against the keys the paper cites.

4 · Two dates for one article

Crossref records an online date and a print year. One article was issued online on 2021-11-02 and printed in 2022; the bibliography cites 2022. Comparing only the first, the check warned on every run. Now either year passes.

5 · A fix the cache undid

Records were cached for 14 days with no format marker, so on the first run after the fix, came from the old cache. Records now carry a schema version, and an older one is fetched again.

All three are wrong inputs: the check ran, but read the wrong thing.

What "verified" means here, and what it does not

  • Lint, offlineevery entry has an identifier; no duplicate keys, dangling citations or orphaned entries; can block a commit
  • Verifylooks each identifier up in arXiv or Crossref and compares title, first author and year; a mismatch is reported as drift
  • Renderedchecks that the keys a paper cites appear in its compiled bibliography

None of these checks that the citing sentence describes the work correctly: % of quotations in medical journals do not (Jergas and Baethge, 2015).

During the study the checker was a script named bibcheck; it became dokimasia, a plugin in techne, on October 7, 2026.

Verification here means the identifier resolves to the cited title, author and year.
