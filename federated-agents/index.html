My idea: a shared add-on, a private add-on, and the company decides

The company marks which of its repositories may feed the shared add-on, and only those do.

Field terms: the add-ons are LoRA adapters; training them together this way is federated learning.

What is the federation, concretely?

Clients are the parties holding review histories. Each trains LoRA adapters on a frozen shared base model; only adapter weights move, never code and never gradients over raw examples.

A round is: the server broadcasts the shared adapter, each client trains locally, each uploads only the adapter its provenance rule marks shareable, the server aggregates and rebroadcasts.

What is not federated: the private adapter, which never leaves, and the provenance rule itself, which the organization sets and audits locally.

Back to the questions

How is each lesson built and tested, step by step?

The add-on is a LoRA adapter (rank ); the score is the real fix's mean log-probability per token, on every token and on the tokens outside names. Every filtering rule is published at ajbarea.github.io/sphragis/dataset.

Back to the questions

What if companies use different or closed models?

The add-ons need a base model whose weights every party can run and train against, so the federation is for companies that host an open-weight model. A company that buys a closed model by the token cannot carry the shared add-on.

Its route is the written one: a rules file or instructions the closed model reads. An exploratory arm measures that route beside the adapters (see the rules-file question).

Back to the questions

What guarantee can be offered about leakage?

None yet, and saying otherwise would be the mistake this work exists to correct.

What exists is a measurement: given a boundary, does the part that crosses it still identify its source? The published boundaries go through it.

A guarantee needs a mechanism with a proof, and the candidates are secure aggregation and differential privacy. Both are measured here rather than assumed.

Back to the questions

What are the pre-registered test and the privacy attacks?

The test: does an add-on trained on one half of an organization's projects beat one trained on the other half, on the first half's unseen months? It passes only if, in OpenStack, Wikimedia and Qt, it clears zero under its registered rule on the whole fix and without its names. The rule and the sealed test months were fixed before any test result existed.

The second contrast: on Qt's C++, does an add-on from a sibling slice of Qt beat one trained on as much C++ from Chromium, another organization? It is the registered test of whether an organization carries style beyond its projects, in this one case. Fuchsia, a second foreign organization, is read beside it, exploratory.

The attacks: does the shared part still reveal its source?

  • Source attribution: which organization did this add-on come from?
  • Min-loss attack: assign an example to the source whose add-on fits it best (the baseline).
  • Aggregate attack: read the combined update, not one organization's.
  • Gradient disaggregation: undo secure aggregation.

A measurement, not a guarantee.

Back to the questions

Is the data sound?

Labels are not inferred. An example is a code hunk, the reviewer comments anchored to it, and the author's next revision, taken directly from the review server.

  • Author comments are excluded, so the signal is a reviewer's, not a self-edit.
  • Near-duplicates are removed before the split; leftover overlap is reported.
  • Label quality is audited blind, by two model raters and a human. On OpenStack's audited lessons, the rater the human checked finds about with a rewrite that answers its comment; the rest stay in and are scored in every arm, so they mostly dilute a difference rather than favor one side.
  • Every filtering rule and what it removed is published: ajbarea.github.io/sphragis/dataset
  • Contamination: every month of data starts after the model's weights were uploaded, and three methods compare it with a window from before. About of development fixes were already in their project's code then; a secondary reading drops them, and AI-disclosing changes.
  • Everything derived is pseudonymized, with addresses swept from comments. RIT's human-subjects office ruled in writing that this is not human-subjects research.

Back to the questions

Does public code review stand in for a private team's?

It is the only review history anyone can study openly, so every claim here is scoped to public communities. Many of their contributors are company and foundation engineers working under required review, which makes them the closest public stand-in.

Whether private teams hold their conventions the same way is untested, and no company publishes how much of its practice stays private. The federation is how that data would be used without anyone having to share it.

Back to the questions

Aren't conventions really the reviewers', and don't they change?

Partly, and the study checks it. Nearly every development example shares a reviewer with the other half of its own organization, so a reviewer effect would show up as the organization's advantage over a foreign one; the study also measures how many reviewers Qt shares with Chromium. An analysis planned before the test months open asks whether that advantage grows with how much of the training data the example's own reviewers wrote. Review is concentrated: in OpenStack, Wikimedia and Qt alike, the busiest tenth of reviewers are credited with about half the training examples.

People also come and go. The test months come after the training months, so conventions that changed with the people count against the result rather than for it.

Back to the questions

How does aggregation work across different adapters?

An update is stored as two small matrices whose product is the real change. Averaging the two separately and multiplying afterwards gives a different answer, so the server has to combine the products.

Clients may also hold different ranks. Existing work covers both: exact aggregation of the product, and rank-heterogeneous averaging.

That choice is a design item with a stated criterion, not an open question, and it is the one place this work picks from the field rather than adding to it.

Back to the questions

Is the dataset useful beyond this one model?

Yes. An example is a reviewer's comment, the code it was about, and the developer's real next revision. Nothing in it comes from a model, so any model or agent can be trained on it or scored against it.

The communities span several languages: OpenStack's examples are led by Python, Wikimedia's by PHP and JavaScript, Qt's by C++, and the two GitHub members, Apache and .NET, add Java, C# and more.

Back to the questions

Would a different model do better?

Dashed line: the corpus begins, October 2024. Filled: trained before it, so a fairer test. Hollow: may have seen the test data.

More models buy generality, not a better answer: the question is whether a team's habits can be learned at all, and a model that may have read the test code cannot settle that. A second family, exploratory, checks whether it is a Qwen quirk.

Compressing the base model to 4 bits (QLoRA) saves memory and at best matches quality, so the main test does not use it. It is planned for the privacy half, because the deployment being tested uses it.

Back to the questions

Would showing the model past reviews do the same?

That is the cheapest competitor: no training, just the most similar past reviews pasted into the prompt. It is registered as an exploratory arm beside the adapters: the base model given the one or three closest past reviews from its own half, the sibling half or another organization, on the same examples.

It is scored by exact match, the study's secondary measure, and binds no verdict. It ran on the development months over the first splits, but exact match missed a planted house rule in most runs, so those readings cannot show whether retrieval learns a convention. The sealed test reports it beside the adapters.

Back to the questions

Why not just write the rules in a file, like AGENTS.md?

That is how coding agents take conventions today: rules files (AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, Cursor rules), organization instructions and memory. They hold what someone wrote down.

What no study I found measures is whether they capture a team's conventions. A 2026 study found developer-written context files helped agents slightly (not significantly), generated ones did not, and both cost about a fifth more inference (Gloaguen et al., arXiv:2602.11988); it does not ask whether the code follows the team's habits.

So an exploratory arm, registered beside the adapters, adds a rules file: the base model given the organization's written conventions, or a file distilled from each half's review comments, given own, sibling or foreign files and scored by exact match, the study's secondary measure, binding no verdict. Qwen3.6-27B distills it, since the 7B model invented rules and citations; a rule must cite two or more comments and share most of its words with one. Like retrieval, its development readings on exact match cannot settle it; the sealed test reports it.

Back to the questions

How is this different from other shared-and-private agent work?

Splitting what agents learn into shared and private parts is no longer new. Federated Agent Optimization (Yang et al., arXiv:2610.01195) defines the field; FedWorld (arXiv:2608.01561) sorts rules into shared, cluster and private by the evidence clients give for and against them; ShareMem and FederatedSkill split memories and skills by an LLM's judgment.

Each decides the split by evidence on a server or an LLM's judgment, not by the owner declaring it from where each example came from. To my knowledge, three things here are new:

  • The owner declares the boundary before training, by where each example came from, and that declaration decides which examples may teach the shared part.
  • Tacit review conventions are what is learned, with a pre-registered test of whether they transfer between halves of an organization. No study I know of tests whether learned style transfers more from an organization's other projects than from a foreign organization; the report tests that in Qt, against Chromium.
  • The shared part is audited by attribution: can someone tell which organization it came from?

Back to the questions

Could it train an agent that checks another agent?

In principle, yes, and it is a later use rather than this study. The label audit already grades whether a developer's revision answers the reviewer's comment (answers it, partly, not actionable, an unrelated rewrite, or needs more context). That is the judgment a checking agent would make about a coding agent's change before a human sees it.

Back to the questions

Is the tool only for code review?

Collection, pseudonymization and the sealed time split work for any review system the tool has a route into (Gerrit and GitHub today). Pairing a comment with the change that answered it, and the cleaning rules, are specific to code review, because the research question is.
